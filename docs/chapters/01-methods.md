# Methods

Alternative history needs discipline. Without it, the phrase can become a license for fantasy, nostalgia, or suspicion without evidence. With discipline, it becomes a way to see how history is made.

This project starts from theory and criticism because those traditions make reading itself visible. They ask what counts as evidence, who gets to interpret, how authority travels, and why some stories become common sense.

## Read Curated Texts Closely

Do not treat sources as data to be harvested. Treat them as arguments, forms, translations, editions, and historical objects. A small number of carefully chosen texts is more useful here than a large pile of loosely related material.

## Read For Provenance

Ask where a source came from, who preserved it, who translated it, who paid for it, who was excluded from it, and how it reached the present.

## Read For Silence

Archives are not just collections of evidence. They are also records of survival, destruction, neglect, and selection. A missing voice can be as historically meaningful as a preserved document.

## Read Across Scales

Large narratives often hide local experience. Local records can also hide the larger structure that made them possible. Move between state papers, oral histories, economic records, literature, court documents, maps, and private testimony.

## Separate Claim Types

- Documented fact: supported by a cited source.
- Interpretation: an argument about what the evidence means.
- Speculation: a labeled possibility, useful for thinking but not proof.
- Myth: a narrative with social power, whether or not it is accurate.

## Use Counterfactuals Carefully

The question "what if?" is useful when it reveals contingency. It is weak when it ignores structure. A good counterfactual asks not only what might have changed, but what forces would have resisted change.
