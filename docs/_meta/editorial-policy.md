# Editorial Policy

Alternative History should be readable, open, rigorous, and curated.

## Core Rules

- Source claims. A reader should be able to trace major factual claims.
- Label speculation. Counterfactual writing is welcome when clearly marked.
- Preserve uncertainty. Do not turn contested evidence into false certainty.
- Avoid sensational framing. "Hidden," "forbidden," and "they do not want you to know" language usually weakens the work.
- Center power without flattening people into symbols.
- Prefer primary sources and strong scholarship over viral summaries.
- Select intentionally. The project is not a general scrape, crawler, or bulk archive.

## Collection Standard

A text belongs in the project when it does at least one of the following:

- Changes how readers understand interpretation, memory, evidence, authorship, translation, or historical narration.
- Gives the book a major theoretical or critical vocabulary.
- Reframes an event, archive, institution, or public memory practice.
- Helps build a deliberate reading path across theory, criticism, and history.

Availability is not enough. A work can be public domain, open access, or easy to extract and still be out of scope.

## Relational Reading

The project should make selected texts come alive together. Do not treat entries as isolated summaries unless isolation is the point.

Strong public pages should show at least one relation:

- A tension between two claims.
- An inheritance across time, language, or tradition.
- A refusal of a premise another text depends on.
- A transformation through commentary, pedagogy, or translation.
- An afterlife in later criticism, politics, or public memory.

## Source Hierarchy

1. Primary sources with clear provenance.
2. Peer-reviewed or university-press scholarship.
3. Critical editions, translations, and anthologies with transparent editorial apparatus.
4. Public-history institutions with transparent sourcing.
5. Reputable journalism and interviews.
6. Informal commentary, used only as a lead or object of analysis.

## Required Sections

Every major entry should include:

- Text, thinker, source, or evidence.
- Context.
- Selection rationale.
- Relation to at least one other text, thinker, or problem.
- What this changes.
- Rights status.
- Open questions.

## Handling Contested Claims

When historians disagree, name the disagreement. Present the strongest versions of the competing interpretations, then explain why the entry takes its position.
